My First Language On leaving China, and learning to trust an unpredictable world through math. ›

P(A|B) = (P(B|A) × P(A)) / P(B)

Written in the language of logic, Bayes’ theorem is a conditional probability axiom that imitates the scientific method, showing us how to update our beliefs based on new evidence. Applied to the bigger canvas of life, it illustrates how we navigate challenges by changing our perspective in light of new information.

Coming to the US from China, I grieved my old home and struggled to adapt. This transcontinental move was my first B factor. Faced with new information, I could remain stagnant or make the best of the change, so I turned towards math — learning to count by gazing at the sky’s infinite sprawl of stars, arithmetic through Chinese card games with my grandma, the Fibonacci by the intricate branching of autumn leaves, the golden ratio by the ocean’s cerulean curve.

Before I learned English, my first language was math. Numbers were my alphabet, the syntax of logic my grammar, its breathtaking proofs my literature. Mathematical certainty was an innate language I spoke fluently without ridicule for my accent, its world of enigmatic Vieta’s theorem and frustrating Fermat’s theorem a laboratory for my endless curiosity. As I dream, tinker, innovate, revere, question, excavate and experiment, I never tire of its mazes, hidden treasures, thrills, and discoveries.

As put in The Elegant Universe, “The boldness of asking deep questions may require unforeseen flexibility if we are to accept the answers.”

I will continue to not only ask but audaciously seek life’s deepest questions. I’ll strive to evolve into a better mathematician, student, and person, growing in coexistence with life’s unpredictable B variables, embracing change with Bayes Theorem. I hope to carry my love for math through college — and who knows, recruit more people into the Bayesian cult.

Linguicide On my father’s hometown, and what a language loses when it dies. ›

Pandas. Dim sum. Great Wall. Those are the most common, striking images conjured when one thinks about China off of the mainstream. When asked to name a language spoken in China they quip back, Mandarin. When asked to name a city that captures the cultural essence of China, most would chirp within a heartbeat, Beijing, Shanghai, or if they were feeling adventurous, Xi’an. But few, if any, would even spare a fleeting breath on the smaller cities on the outskirts of these global players who capture the world’s attention on the social, political, economic market and dominate the image of China in popular media.

Those were my thoughts, standing in the crumbling doorway of my grandma’s house for winter break, an unsettling feeling in my stomach and low expectations of Duqiao, Zhejiang, a small, farming city nestled by piercing green mountains, cradled by the Qiantang river. It’s a city where if you were to trek from one end to the other, you can knock on every random door along the way and fall into easy conversation — either as an acquaintance, or acquaintance of an acquaintance, or if you’re blood (even if you’re the cousin of who knows how many removed) you’re staying, no buts or maybes, for dinner, gossip, and card games. Half of the people living there are in their late sixties to eighties, spending retirement days idly tending overgrown rows of potatoes, tomatoes, cabbage, bok choy, chives, under the lazy sun and generous Zhejiang rains, and the evenings raising the occasional fist at a TV screen — not out of the pressure of scraping yuan to put food on the table, but out of decades-old habit.

Others are second or third generation, easily distinguishable by their faces powdered with blush, branded bags, and unsoiled hands that never worked to tease life out of dirt — desperately trying to widen the distance between the roots that made them and find a way out of the city, to scrub free the dirt of their poor hometown. While natives made up most of the demographic, some had moved from neighboring towns, smelling the entrepreneurial scent of this small farming town’s transformation into an industrial city, too poor themselves to move straight to Shanghai.

In the span of a few decades, the mom-and-pop restaurants that sold a colorful array of cultural treats born right on that soil were being replaced by newer stores, sometimes wiping out two or three family restaurants at once. Electrically-powered storefronts stank of pride, modernness, and superiority, their polished doors gliding noiselessly as customers filed into sterile, air-conditioned rooms to eat under a multi-colored TV opera show. As money lined new pockets, the elderly couple next door were closing down, boxes stacked in front, no longer selling the hometown special — Duqiao ginger, clam noodles. When I asked about the shutdown, a sharp pang echoing in my heart, they only shared a long, forlorn look that spoke of volumes of history about to end. A long street was entirely swapped out for Western brands — McDonald’s, a local favorite, and KFC, frequented by whining toddlers and placating parents. Eventually, as the pressures of living forced their hand, some would reluctantly dismiss their cultural comfort food for more popular dishes — one store, one trend at a time — until these specialties no longer held the same fond warmth and meaning in people’s lives. These mom-and-pop stores float barely on the surface, kept alive by the older generation’s loyalty, many already shoved out by new Western brands and the tide of modernization.

When my dad peered wistfully at the schoolchildren jabbering in Mandarin outside his old elementary school, he told me how they used to teach the local tongue here, with its own swirling accent — the language that held the history and culture of this little but vibrant town. Now Mandarin was the norm; the local tongue was carried on only by relatives, and even they were reluctant to pass it further, their own children more enticed by the romance of Western languages, faces twisting sourly at the idea of devoting time to a dialect whose speakers don’t amount to even a single percent of the population. Traditions like climbing the hilltop each year to honor our ancestors with rice, wine, and flowers were ground into oblivion by the busyness of modern life; waking at dawn to light incense and set out a feast of auspicious foods became more inconvenience than devotion, its sentimental value slowly forgotten. Meanwhile Christmas meals and Halloween costumes, deemed cool and trendy, were on the rise among more and more kids in the neighborhood.

My dad told me all this, his face a mesh of feelings warring against each other, as we strolled through his old town, his childhood home. We walked past the landmarks of his childhood, old memories surfacing through years of dust, and bone-deep losses that chill to the core upon realizing they are now a remnant of the past in a changing present. The stream that held so many beautiful memories of languid summer evenings for my father now ran black. The candy shop run by an old woman blind in one eye, who always slipped my dad an extra piece, had been shut down for a booming arcade business.

Duqiao is a place of ironies, of different time zones and segments of old and new generations mashed together yet somehow running in parallel, conflicted in its dichotomous culture, current life built on the faded rubble of the older generation, its trajectory driven forward by Western trends and media definitions of the norm and the cool. Where pizza is served in blue and red boxes next to roasted chestnuts, where tech-obsessed teenagers’ faces are illuminated by dancing figures on TikTok beside elderly people with their foreheads pressed reverently to their palms in prayer. In this catapulting era, space is limited and the need to survive takes precedence: if old traditions don’t churn out profit, the town’s landscape morphs to fit the new trend, because it is simply unrealistic to stay frozen in the past in a constantly shifting world. It makes a kind of logical sense — corporations and institutions of power function on globally recognized languages, on Western languages, especially English, and on popular languages like Mandarin. It’s the language of business and politics, of wealth and open doors, of mainstream and media; keeping in touch with an “insignificant” culture gets deprioritized, as learning the bigger languages becomes a necessity.

But this exact scene is unfolding in the serenity of any arbitrary sleepy town, in every pocket of the world — Malaysia, Pakistan, Israel. Industrialization is inevitable, but in what direction, following what trends? An estimated 14,000 languages will be extinguished by the end of the century — roughly 14 languages a day whose syllables will never be uttered again, whose culture’s rituals can never again be carried out in their sacred tongue, the tie to their traditions and ancestors severed for good. This is linguicide.

Westernization and linguicide are different from industrialization, which is exacerbated by media trends — it’s seeing entire streets wiped out, family businesses shut down, once-fondness and reverence turned into disgust for one’s own identity, centuries-old traditions fading to black in favor of other cultures. It’s witnessing the dying of your own culture. Entire centuries are being wiped into an oblivious smudge as languages continuously disappear from the face of the Earth — the Cherokee tribe from North Carolina, the Judezmo language spoken in Serbia and Morocco, the Rapa language of French Polynesia. Some are already extinct.

Languages are sacred; they are the vehicle for immersing in a culture. Losing them means far more than losing a set of an alphabet — it means losing an entire realm of tribal music and art, of fireside myths and superstitions, of an ancient religion and rich culture, of an essential, beautiful voice in our global community, lost forever. The majority of these are minority languages, spoken in countries of color, whose people are the ones absorbing the blow — forced to learn the language not of their blood and land, but of their oppressors.

The world of media falls easily into single-narrative labeling, for lack of representation and coverage of minority groups and ethnic cultures — overplaying the dominant voice until it becomes the “norm,” the mainstream, the trend, and everything else becomes eccentric by contrast. From the movies we watch, to the severe lack of representation in Hollywood, to the chaos of social media, these platforms feed into one another to create this disparity. In the sphere of power, we see the same game of politics played out — the same stories of oppression and trauma written into this soil.

While Westernization is a global trend, inspecting each country shows a gradual transition toward a culturally homogeneous society, as Indigenous and local languages are swept out in favor of a country’s official language — the pressure to conform, and the need to thrive, too great. Smaller, minority, Indigenous, island, and local languages and cultures are continuously swallowed by more powerful languages of commerce and convenience, caught in a forever tug of war over shifting power. The 52 ethnic languages of China — the Uyghurs of Xinjiang, the Zhuang of Guangxi — will soon be sounds and songs of the past.

Media and art are beautiful tools: a global movie screen and a conversation space for seven billion people to share, inform, educate, inspire, and advocate about their different opinions, upbringings, and cultures. It’s beautiful at its core. But we need to use that global sphere to bring in more minority voices, to introduce the world to the beautiful languages and cultures disappearing at the brink of extinction — to let people feel admiration and respect rather than cold aloofness, to give these languages another breath of life. The dying of language is directly linked to the dying of culture; media can be changed to incorporate more ethnically diverse content to inspire curiosity and broaden acceptance. But on the governmental level, policy needs to protect these endangered languages too — much like endangered species. These cultures and languages are vital to our global ecosystem.

The days trickle by, impossible to snatch back and savor slowly, like a drifting dandelion. Soon, in three days, I’ll be leaving this small, but far from quiet, town full of stories and stories yet to be told. Now, when prompted to think about China, I think of evenings roasting sweet yams with my grandma as she told me of her childhood. I think of farmers from miles away pedaling bike rickshaws to sell produce still sprinkled with soil, freshly plucked. I think of steaming bowls of ginger, clam noodles my aunt sells in her store, smiling at me as she scoops extra clams with her chopsticks into mine. When urged to name a language, I think of the haggling voices, the beautiful opera singing, the musical language of Duqiao. When asked about a culture, I close my eyes, lost in the maudlin haze and emotion of memory, thinking about the winter break I took here that changed me fundamentally — my principles, and my thinking about all things big and small.

The Empty Fort On Zhuge Liang, wit, and a bloodless battle. ›

The spring wind swept its warm breath across the peaceful courtyard. The eerie notes of a lute, plucked from a practiced hand, crescendoed into the lifting wind. Commoners dressed in Taoist robes swept fallen leaves, completing the picture of tranquility.

Little could anyone know that the air stirred with unrest: the hooves of an approaching army, thousands strong, led by the formidable Sima Yi, descending like locusts on Xicheng. But seeing the idyll of the city wall, Sima Yi surveyed the scene with growing suspicion. He assumed it was a trick, that beneath this vigil lay a bloodbath of ambush and traps, so thoroughly laid that no defense was needed. Smug with his own cleverness, he turned and left the fortress unscathed, certain that anyone who dared enter was as good as dead.

The Empty Fort Strategy.

Zhuge Liang made a risky gamble, but his ultimate victory showed that in a game between wit and brute strength, wit wins.

On a personal level, I find his story compelling because in life, as in war, it’s possible to overcome disadvantage by intelligently playing the cards you’re dealt. Although in his position, I’d most likely scream the living daylights out and burrow six feet under.

I’d draw advice and inspiration from Zhuge Liang to confidently face challenges, remembering that with determination, wit can triumph against the odds, in life and international relations alike.

The Birdbath On my mother, a stone bird, and the singer she almost became. ›

In my old house there lived a ghost family. In the attic I found albums coated in dust, memories carved into the grooves of the walls — the leftover shape of someone else who’d called this place home. But the strangest thing they left behind was a birdbath, carved of white stone, with a bird forever caught mid-flight.

You used to stare at it during the long hours we were at school, hustling around the kitchen — breakfast, lunch, dinner, clean, repeat, seven days a week, fifty-two weeks a year. Through the narrow window above the sink, that birdbath was your muse, the one thing you studied through years of loneliness and endless chores. I still wonder whether you saw yourself as the bird trapped in stone, or the one that flew away — away from home, away from the futures you could have had.

As the hours burned into sunset, you’d slide a thin disc into the radio and let the old Chinese songs spill out, your voice rich as wine, dancing through the notes like it hadn’t aged a day. But inevitably a cry, a question, a conversation would break it — and the dream in your eyes would fade, your soapy hands going still as the spell of the past let you go. You’d touch a shaking hand to your cheek, remembering that you weren’t a singer, that was a teenage girl’s dream thirty years ago, that you were a mother now, raising four children far from home. Your eyes would turn to marble and you’d attack the dishes with new force, scrubbing away the past along with the plates, the water splashing off in white foam, carrying with it the residue of a career and independence you never got to have.

The bird that got away and the bird that stayed were the same bird, I think. And still you hummed — notes of something flying free, notes of a dream that came true a different way.

“You sing beautifully,” I told you once, my voice cracking, the room blurring with tears. I laced my fingers through yours, tracing the winding lines of your palm, the wrinkles earned by endurance and strength.

Thank you, Mom.

Xue-xi, Zhu-ren, You-yi On the three principles I keep circling back to. ›

What defines a leader? I keep circling the question, most as the oldest of four. I’ve found myself returning to three Confucian principles: xue-xi, knowledge; zhu-ren, service; you-yi, connection.

“They who learn are not equal to those who love learning, and they who love learning are not equal to those who delight in it.” Passionate learning. I want a compass, not just a destination — curiosity that never gets lost because it never runs out of places to go.

“If you want happiness for an hour, take a nap. If you want happiness for a year, inherit a fortune. If you want happiness for a lifetime, help somebody.” Service to others, especially the mentors and communities that shaped me.

“A single conversation with a wise person is worth a month’s study of books.” Meaningful connection — the kind that widens a worldview one conversation at a time.

I don’t know if I’ll ever be sure I’m a good leader. But these three keep asking the question back at me, which might be the point.

A Handshake, Two Hemispheres On Thich Nhat Hanh, Martin Luther King, and the quiet strength of peace. ›

In 1966, Thich Nhat Hanh, a Vietnamese Buddhist monk, and Martin Luther King Jr. met and shook hands to condemn the Vietnam War — two hemispheres, bound by a shared vision of a future built on nonviolence and compassion instead of the war-torn present around them. Their handshake is proof that humanity binds us stronger than our differences, and that peace has a quiet, prevailing strength in times of unrest. Equity doesn’t happen overnight, but the least we can do is share a moment of it.

The Queen Who Wouldn’t Watch On Queen Liliʻuokalani’s boycott of Hawaii’s annexation. ›

On July 7, 1898, the flag of the Kingdom of Hawaii came down for the last time. Queen Liliʻuokalani refused to watch, sequestering herself in her own palace rather than attend the ceremony that ended her nation, then imprisoned there afterward by the American businessmen who profited from it. Her boycott didn’t stop the annexation. But her eight days of imprisonment, and the words she left behind, outlasted it — a testament to quiet defiance, and a voice for every land taken the same way.

Generational Miscommunication On technology and the distance it opens between generations. ›

Generational communication, strained by digitalization. Cultural, ideological, and political distances grow insuperable against technology’s exponential curve. Modern devices become exclusive to the young; elderly communities are isolated by their own unfamiliarity with them. Polarization deepens, progress stagnates, ethical guardrails get bypassed entirely. We need technology built for every age, and a little more patience between generations.

Endangerment of Math-Art On what math education loses when it forgets it’s art. ›

Math education, artful and endangered. Reduced to memorization and a ladder of competition, it’s built to mint workers and GDP, not wonder. Along the way it enforces incompetence on girls and minorities, and calls it natural. But math is art — it’s in the ocean’s Fibonacci curl, the lyricism of a good proof. This is a dying, sacred thing.

Overstimulation On the information firework, and what it costs us. ›

We crave stimulation, need to stake our presence, need to know the world hasn’t moved on without us — addicted to the firework of information. Family memories get punctured by pings; moments of human interaction get lost to the scroll. There’s beauty in life’s smaller gifts. In a world, every so often, unplugged.

Media Desensitization On grief turned into ear-candy. ›

Every day the news bleeds, and every day we get better at not flinching. Death tolls follow cooking videos in the same feed. Grief becomes ear-candy; sympathy becomes selfish relief. What isn’t personal doesn’t feel urgent, so it goes unhelped — and inequity gets to hide behind our indifference. The world is connected by our humanity before anything else. We fight desensitization with empathy, on purpose, over and over.

Boredom On stalling the subway train on purpose. ›

Boredom is the antithesis of modern life, so we hurry past it — past the street corner, past the pause, past the moments that don’t announce themselves. Somewhere along the way we started measuring joy in numbers, color, and fame. Life moves like a subway train, but sometimes it’s worth stalling it on purpose, just to sit in the boredom for a while.
